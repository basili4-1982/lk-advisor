# Human-in-the-loop review of AI-generated relations, entity matches and KG edges

*Scope: public sources as of Nov 2026. Context of use: LLM-generated and co-purchase-derived product relations in a large, long-tail catalog, where a human must confirm both the semantic relation type and its safety. No client data used.*

---

## How do Diffbot, Graphwise, Zingg, Senzing, Tamr, Neo4j LLM Graph Builder, GraphRAG and similar tools generate candidate links and present them for review?

### Takeaway
There are two distinct product philosophies, and the split matters more than any UI detail. **Fully automatic graph builders** (Diffbot, Microsoft GraphRAG, Neo4j LLM Graph Builder) generate links by model/heuristic and expose **no review surface at all** — they merge on score or on string identity and publish. **Entity-resolution/MDM platforms** (Senzing, Zingg, Tamr) all converge on the same three-part architecture: candidate generation → scored decision with a *review band* between auto-accept and auto-reject → a reviewer-facing queue, with the queue fed by score thresholds or by change-detection rather than by raw volume.

### Cited Findings
- Diffbot describes its knowledge graph as "an automatically constructed knowledge graph (not based on human labor)", rebuilt by "a fully automatic pipeline every 4-5 days without human supervision" — human curation is framed as the thing being replaced, not a step in the loop. — [Diffbot blog](https://blog.diffbot.com/diffbots-approach-to-knowledge-graph/); [Diffbot KG glossary](https://blog.diffbot.com/knowledge-graph-glossary/)
- Microsoft GraphRAG merges extracted subgraphs by **exact string identity**: "any entities with the same *title* and *type* are merged by creating an array of their descriptions. Similarly, any relationships with the same *source* and *target* are merged." There is no confidence score, no candidate set and no review step in the documented default dataflow. — [GraphRAG default dataflow](https://microsoft.github.io/graphrag/index/default_dataflow/)
- This is a known weakness rather than a design choice: GraphRAG-style pipelines "typically rely on string-matching heuristics to merge similar entities, leaving many duplicates unresolved", and without entity resolution the graph degenerates into "a union of subgraphs with no cross edges". — [Less is More: Denoising Knowledge Graphs for RAG (arXiv 2510.14271)](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14271v1)
- Neo4j LLM Graph Builder has no documented review queue; its duplicate handling is a **post-hoc automated pass** controlled by two env vars: `DUPLICATE_SCORE_VALUE` (default `0.97`, "Node score value to match duplicate nodes") and `DUPLICATE_TEXT_DISTANCE` (default `5`, "find distance for all node pairs in the graph"). Schema configuration is the documented lever for quality: "higher quality data extraction if you configure the graph schema for nodes and relationship types". — [Neo4j LLM Graph Builder repo](https://github.com/neo4j-labs/llm-graph-builder); [Neo4j Labs product page](https://neo4j.com/labs/genai-ecosystem/llm-graph-builder/)
- Neo4j's own guidance concedes the consequence: knowledge graphs "have been plagued by duplicate nodes that dilute the power of their graph. Even the best technical teams have had difficulty getting these duplicate nodes resolved." — [Neo4j, Entity resolved knowledge graphs](https://neo4j.com/blog/developer/entity-resolved-knowledge-graphs/)
- **Senzing** generates candidates via *candidate keys* derived from record features: "Values shared by too many entities, such as a bank's toll-free phone number already associated with hundreds of people, go generic and are not used to find candidates. The remaining keys are used to retrieve candidate entities, because entities must find each other through candidate keys before they can be scored." Each record is then scored against each candidate entity and passed through *principles* that classify it as "the same as, possibly the same as, or possibly related to" the candidate — a native three-way output that maps directly onto auto-accept / review / reject. — [Senzing, How ER works](https://senzing.com/how-entity-resolution-works-with-senzing/)
- Senzing presents this to a human via `sz_explorer`, a terminal UI with `search` → `compare` (side-by-side entity view) → `why` (why two entities did *not* resolve) → `how` (the step-by-step resolution path of one entity). The docs explicitly position this as the substrate for customer-facing review: "Incorporate searching and retrieving records into workflows for making decisions, or into a UI to display entities to end users and allow them to ask `why` and `how`." — [Senzing, Entity Exploration](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_basic_exploration/)
- **Zingg** generates candidates in a distinct phase from scoring: `findTrainingData` "samples candidate pairs from the blocking output, prioritizing pairs where the model's current confidence is lowest", then `label` presents them one pair at a time. — [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning); [Zingg docs, Create Training Data](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/running-zingg/create-training-data)
- Zingg's production routing is explicitly tiered by score: "**High-confidence matches** … route directly to the golden record or identity graph without human review… **Mid-confidence matches** route to a review queue… **Low-confidence or unusual clusters** flag for inspection." — [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning)
- **Tamr**'s patented review application is the most explicit published queue design in this set. Reviewers get a two-region screen: a *clustering pane* listing proposed clusters with summary info, and a *cluster pane* with the records of the selected cluster. Filters include "whether clusters have changes, and if so what type (new cluster, cluster with records added, cluster with records moved to or from other clusters, cluster with records deleted, or retired/deleted cluster); confidence, such as high/medium/low or a custom range; and various statuses, including assignment or lock condition." Clusters can be sorted by "name, number of record, assigned status, locked status, and confidence". — [Tamr patent US11321359B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11321359B2/en)
- Tamr's 2025–26 direction is agent-mediated: a "Curation Agent compares the outputs of entity matches and explains the reasoning behind why records are (or are not) a match"; background agents "continuously scan for outliers, duplicates, inconsistencies… then escalate exceptions into **prioritized suggestion queues** so data stewards can route and resolve issues efficiently". — [Tamr FAQ](https://www.tamr.com/faq); [Tamr Curator Hub press release](https://www.tamr.com/press-releases/tamr-unveils-curator-hub-to-solve-the-hardest-part-of-data-quality-and-curation)
- Tamr's clustering is ML-based ("modified random forest and bottom-up average linkage") combined with "human feedback from data curators with pre-trained machine learning (ML) models and semantic comparison with large language models (LLMs)". — [Tamr, AI-native approach](https://cloud.docs.tamr.com/docs/about-tamrs-ai-native-approach)
- **Graphwise (Ontotext + Semantic Web Company)** positions entity resolution as a component of a "context layer" and sells GraphDB as the store plus semantic enrichment (entity linking, tagging) on top; I found **no public documentation of a reviewer-facing candidate queue** for GraphDB itself. — [Graphwise, What Is a Context Layer](https://graphwise.ai/en/fundamentals/what-is-a-context-layer-ai/); [Ontotext, Using Entity Linking](https://www.ontotext.com/blog/using-entity-linking-to-turn-your-graph-into-a-knowledge-graph/)
- The scale argument for not reviewing everything: "A catalog of even a few million listings implies a pairwise comparison space on the order of trillions of candidate pairs, which is computationally infeasible" — blocking/candidate generation is mandatory before any review step. — [A Multi-Modal Entity Resolution Architecture For Product Catalog (IJAIML)](https://www.svedbergopen.com/index.php/ijaiml/article/download/3096/1928/6869) *(abstract snippet only; full PDF not retrievable)*

### Inferences
- The absence of review UI in Diffbot/GraphRAG/LLM-Graph-Builder is a *product posture*, not an oversight: they optimize for zero-touch graph construction and accept duplicate/false-merge debt. Any product-relation system that must confirm semantic type and safety cannot copy that posture — it needs the ER-platform architecture (candidate → banded score → queue) even though its "entities" are products and its "edges" are typed relations.
- The reusable primitive across Senzing/Zingg/Tamr is **not** a UI but a *banded decision*: three zones (auto-accept / human / auto-reject) with thresholds calibrated on labeled pairs. For relations, the analogue is per-relation-type bands, because "is a substitute for" and "is an accessory for" have different error costs.
- Candidate generation and review presentation are cleanly separable. Senzing shows you can keep a deterministic, auditable matcher (no LLM) and still drive a review UI purely off `why`/`how` output — useful when safety review must be explainable to a non-ML auditor.
- Tamr's filter vocabulary (change type, confidence band, assignment, lock) is a better template for a long-tail catalog than a plain score sort: most items are unchanged, so *diffing against the last published state* is what keeps a queue small.

### Gaps
- No public screenshots or docs found for a **Graphwise/Ontotext** candidate-review workflow; their public material covers entity linking and context layers, not steward queues.
- Diffbot publishes no accuracy figures, no false-merge rate, and no correction/feedback API for its KG; "no human supervision" is asserted, not measured, in the sources I could reach.
- I could not retrieve the full text of the product-catalog ER paper (paywall/PDF-only), so its architecture claims are only supported at abstract level.

## What confidence and evidence is shown to reviewers? How are uncertain, conflicting or duplicate suggestions handled?

### Takeaway
Mature tools never show a bare probability. They show **(a) a score, (b) the feature-level reasons with positive *and* negative contributions, and (c) a structural/aggregate signal about the cluster** (density, centrality, determinism). Uncertainty is handled by an explicit third state ("can't say" / "possible match"), and duplicates are handled by making the *unit of judgment* a group boundary rather than a pair. Zingg's 2026 post-mortem is the sharpest public statement of that last point.

### Cited Findings
- Zingg warns that the score is not a probability: "A score of 0.6 is not necessarily '60% likely to be a match' — it's a point on a learned curve shaped by your data." The classifier "finds the best curve… this score is dependent on the individual features but is not a linear function but a curve fit." — [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning)
- Zingg's review surface is pair-level and evidence-level: `explain --zinggid <id>` returns "the pairs it matched, their similarity scores and how the cluster formed", rendered as an explicit pair matrix (`pk1`, `pk2`, `match`), e.g. A104–A211 0.94, A104–A339 "rule", A211–A339 0.87, A339–A402 0.71, A402–A517 0.92. — [Zingg Explainability](https://www.zingg.ai/product/explainability)
- It also surfaces the transitivity problem directly to the reviewer: "A104 and A517 never matched each other. They share a ZINGG_ID because the matches chain through A339 and A402." — same source
- Cluster-level review signals are published as named metrics: `z_cluster_centrality` ("matched pairs divided by all possible pairs, n(n-1)/2. Near 1 is dense; low values are sparse clusters worth a look"), `z_cluster_determinism` ("share of possible pairs connected by deterministic rules"), `z_record_centrality` ("a record with unusually high centrality can falsely bridge two entities. Check it before it merges them"), `z_record_determinism`. — [Zingg Explainability](https://www.zingg.ai/product/explainability); [Zingg docs, Explainability and Statistics](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/interpreting-results/explainability-and-statistics)
- Output columns designed for triage: `Z_MINSCORE` ("weakest match confidence in the cluster") and `Z_MAXSCORE`; Zingg advises inspecting clusters with `z_minScore` of 0 and clusters larger than 4–5 members. — [Zingg docs, Interpret Output Scores](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/interpreting-results/interpret-output-scores); [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning)
- **Senzing** shows feature-level evidence with *negative* contributions: in `why`, "The `MATCH_KEY` shows the list of features that contributed to the match, both positively and negatively. The principle is also displayed and is the actual reason for the match." A disagreeing DOB field is rendered in red "because it scored 58 and detracted from the match". The lower pane shows "the keys that placed them on a short list of candidates". — [Senzing, Entity Exploration](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_basic_exploration/)
- Senzing keeps three graded outcomes rather than a binary: match / **possible match** / **possible relationship**, where "If entities score below the resolution threshold, they may still be related." Snapshot reports count "Ambiguous Matches — entities where a record could plausibly belong to more than one entity", "Possible Matches — entities sharing important attributes but with some disagreements", and "Possible Relationships — entities related through lesser attributes like shared addresses". — [Senzing, How ER works](https://senzing.com/how-entity-resolution-works-with-senzing/); [Senzing, Snapshot Analysis](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_snapshot/)
- Senzing's explanations are rule-based and explicitly non-LLM ("Senzing does not use large language models"), which is why each step can enumerate "the scores of all compared features, along with the match key and the principle that was satisfied". — [Senzing, How ER works](https://senzing.com/how-entity-resolution-works-with-senzing/)
- **Tamr** computes two confidence directions, not one: "intra-cluster confidence for each cluster through pairwise similarity of record fields across all pairs of data records within a cluster, and **inter-cluster confidence** for each cluster through pairwise similarity… selected to pair data records in one cluster with data records from a different cluster." This is what lets a reviewer see "this cluster is tight, but it is barely distinguishable from the neighbouring cluster" — the classic product-variant failure. — [Tamr patent US11321359B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11321359B2/en)
- Tamr's agent layer is used specifically to supply *reasons*: "Curators see the reasoning that's provided before they confirm or reject changes", and the Curation Agent "explains the reasoning behind why records are (or are not) a match". — [Tamr FAQ](https://www.tamr.com/faq)
- **Neo4j Agent Memory** documents the two-threshold policy and its limits: automatic merge at `resolution.auto_merge_threshold` = 0.90 by default, review at `resolution.review_threshold` = 0.85. "Raising the automatic threshold reduces the set of candidates merged without review; moving the review threshold changes which ambiguous pairs reach the queue. **The thresholds are score cutoffs, not guaranteed precision percentages.**" — [Neo4j Agent Memory, Resolution and deduplication](https://neo4j.com/labs/agent-memory/explanation/resolution-deduplication/)
- That same doc gives a product-catalog worked example of exactly the semantic trap in scope: "'Apple iPhone 15 Pro' and 'iPhone 15 Pro'" = possible aliases needing model+capacity+color+catalog ID; "'iPhone 15 Pro' and 'iPhone 15 Pro Max'" = "Distinct variants / Keep separate"; "'Nike Air Max' and 'Nike Air Max 90'" = "Product family versus a specific model / Do not merge without a more specific identity". It adds: "A shared family name, brand, or description is evidence of **relatedness, not necessarily identity**." — [Neo4j Agent Memory](https://neo4j.com/labs/agent-memory/explanation/resolution-deduplication/)
- The cost asymmetry is stated plainly: "Merging two product variants can mix inventory or prices… A resolution policy should consider the cost of a false merge as well as the cost of missed matches." — same source
- **Zingg's** 2026 engineering post reframes the unit of review: "a steward doesn't really judge a record in isolation. They judge a **boundary**: these records are one real-world entity, and those aren't. The unit of human judgment is the group, not the row… Shown five records, a steward might say three are one person and two are another. That judgment is about where the line falls, and a per-row flag has nowhere to put it." — [Zingg, A Human Corrected the AI](https://www.zingg.ai/post/human-corrected-the-ai-then-the-data-changed)
- **Diffbot** surfaces evidence only as retrieval metadata — search results carry a `score` per hit and `content` — not as a merge justification. — [Diffbot](https://www.diffbot.com/)
- **Wikidata** (community curation at scale) handles duplicates with a merge gadget, explicit "mark as duplicate", sitelink-conflict rules, and a *permanent* escape hatch: `instance of: Wikimedia duplicated page` + qualifier `said to be the same as`, or `permanent duplicated item (P2959)` as "a last resort". — [Wikidata Help:Merge](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Merge)

### Inferences
- For product relations, the "confidence" a reviewer needs is not the model's probability but a **decomposed evidence bundle**: which attributes matched (title tokens, brand, category, size/capacity), which *contradicted* (different variant suffix, different pack size), and which relation-typed cue fired (co-purchase lift, LLM rationale sentence, shared accessory taxonomy node). Senzing's red-scored disagreeing field and Zingg's pair matrix are the two cheapest proven renderings of this.
- The `z_cluster_centrality` / `z_record_centrality` idea transfers almost unchanged to relation review as a **hub/bridge detector**: a product that "explains" hundreds of proposed edges is either a genuine accessory hub or a false bridge (e.g. a generic "compatible with" claim). Flagging high-centrality nodes is cheaper than reviewing edges one by one.
- Inter-cluster confidence (Tamr) has a direct product analogue that most tools lack: the reviewer should see the *runner-up* candidate. "This is the closest alternative relation target, and here is why we ranked it second" is what prevents variant/family errors, which are the dominant semantic failure mode in catalogs.
- Treating "duplicate" as a first-class, non-destructive state (Wikidata's `said to be the same as`, Senzing's "possible match", Zingg's "can't say") is strictly better than a binary merge for a long-tail catalog, because the correct answer is often "same product family, different SKU" — an edge, not a merge.

### Gaps
- No public source quantifies how much a feature-level explanation improves reviewer accuracy or speed in ER specifically; the evidence is design-documented, not A/B-tested, in vendor material.
- Graphwise/Ontotext: no public documentation of what evidence (score, matched attributes, provenance) is shown in a review surface.
- No vendor publishes calibration data (reliability diagrams) for their match scores, so "0.9" cannot be compared across tools.

## What queue designs work: one-at-a-time, pairwise comparison, bulk approve, filters, sampling, second reviewer, escalation?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a **layered queue**, not a single mode: bulk/filtered approval for homogeneous low-risk changes, group-level (not row-level) decisions for cluster boundaries, pairwise one-at-a-time only for the genuinely ambiguous tail, and a sampled control stream to measure what the other lanes miss. Second review should be a *measured sample* (≈10–20% overlap) plus mandatory adjudication for high-stakes items, not blanket double-review.

### Cited Findings
- **Pairwise, one at a time (active learning lane).** Zingg's `label` phase presents exactly two records and three buttons: `Record 1: John Smith, 12 Oak St… Record 2: J. Smith, 12 Oak Street… Are these the same person? [yes / no / can't say]`. The loop is run "iteratively, typically three to five rounds, until the model's confidence stabilizes", and "30–50 labeled matching pairs is usually sufficient to train to useful accuracy". — [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning); [Zingg docs](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/running-zingg/create-training-data)
- Zingg is explicit that the pairs are *selected*, not sampled: "Zingg selects the most informative pairs from your data, not random samples." — [Zingg docs, Create Training Data](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/running-zingg/create-training-data)
- **Group-level decision.** Zingg's design conclusion: stewards judge boundaries, so "a yes or no on what the machine proposed isn't enough… That judgment is about where the line falls, and a per-row flag has nowhere to put it." — [Zingg, A Human Corrected the AI](https://www.zingg.ai/post/human-corrected-the-ai-then-the-data-changed)
- **What a human decision must lock.** Zingg codified three rules after shipping: "**Members stay put.** No automated process moves a record out of a group a human confirmed… **No automatic merges.** Two human-owned groups never combine on their own… **Growth is allowed.** A confirmed group can still pick up genuinely new records." Plus the asymmetry: "The machine may *add* to a human decision but never *undo* it… if confirming something doesn't make it stick, people stop bothering to confirm things." — same source
- **Bulk approve, scoped by criteria.** Tamr's provenance patent describes bulk approval as conditional and revocable: "Did System Operator 130 approve this linkage element as part of a **bulk approval based on some criteria**? If so, then it is considered valid **as long as the criteria remain true**. If the criteria involve a confidence from a model, and that model becomes invalid, then the model may be recomputed… If the element satisfies the criteria with the new model, then the element remains valid." — [Tamr patent US11042523B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11042523B2/en)
- **Filters as the primary queue-shaping tool.** Tamr filters clusters by change type, confidence band (high/medium/low or custom range), assignment and lock status, and sorts by confidence/record count; the UI uses colour/highlight to mark "new records added… records moving to the cluster from other clusters… records that have been deleted". — [Tamr patent US11321359B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11321359B2/en)
- **Thresholding to keep the queue empty.** Tamr's method explicitly "appl[ies] a threshold setting to the confidence metrics, or degree of change in confidence metrics between a published cluster and matching proposed cluster, to identify clusters warranting manual review, **and excluding clusters not identified as warranting manual review from those requiring approval**". — same patent
- **Escalation / prioritised routing.** Tamr: agents "escalate exceptions into prioritized suggestion queues so data stewards can **route** and resolve issues efficiently within governance policies", with a dashboard view for monitoring. — [Tamr FAQ](https://www.tamr.com/faq); [Curator Hub PR](https://www.tamr.com/press-releases/tamr-unveils-curator-hub-to-solve-the-hardest-part-of-data-quality-and-curation)
- **Sampling + control stream + double review (the measurement lane).** A concrete, capacity-matched queue design: "population: 400 evaluator-flagged traces; queue: all 40 traces from the newest prompt version, random 80 of the remaining failures, **30 unflagged random traces (control sample)**; double-review: **20% overlap**; adjudication: disagreements resolved by a third reviewer each Friday; export: final labels appended to the regression dataset weekly." — [Arize, What Is an Annotation Queue](https://arize.com/glossary/annotation-queue/)
- The control sample is the part "teams most often skip… Without it, you can estimate how often the evaluator's flags are right. You cannot estimate how many failures it missed." And: "A queue is a sampling and workflow decision before it is a tooling feature. Which cases get reviewed determines what the labels can tell you." — same source
- Reviewer count guidance: "Use a single reviewer for routine items, with a deliberate overlap that is double-reviewed to measure agreement. That overlap is often **10-20%**. Items that decide a launch or change an evaluator's threshold deserve full double review plus adjudication." — same source
- **Assignment and capacity control in a real tool.** Argilla distributes work by a per-record minimum response count (`min_submitted`); when met, "the status of the record will change to `completed`, and the record will be removed from the `Pending` queue of all team members". Per-record states are `submitted`, `discarded`, `draft` — so an abandoned judgment is distinguishable from a negative one. — [Argilla docs, Distribute the annotation task](https://docs.argilla.io/latest/how_to_guides/distribution/)
- **Low-confidence + random audit routing as a managed service.** Amazon A2I's documented pattern is "human review of low-confidence predictions or random prediction samples", with support for "multiple reviewers"; note A2I "is no longer open to new customers" and receives no new features, so it is a design reference rather than a build option. — [AWS A2I docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/a2i-use-augmented-ai-a2i-human-review-loops.html)
- **Audit-as-a-queue.** Senzing turns review into a discrepancy triage: `sz_audit` compares results to a truth set and produces two review categories — "`MERGE`: Senzing resolved records into the same entity that the truth set kept separate" and "`SPLIT`: the truth set expected records to resolve together, but Senzing kept them as separate entities" — then lets the reviewer drill from category → `MATCH_KEY` → "the specific records involved". It stresses "These are not necessarily errors." — [Senzing, Auditing](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_audit/)
- **Reversibility as a queue feature.** Wikidata documents unmerging as a normal operation, and provides the `permanent duplicated item` escape hatch when a merge must never happen. — [Wikidata Help:Merge](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Merge)
- **Diffbot has no queue by design**: the graph is rebuilt wholesale every 4–5 days, so there is no review lane, no assignment and no escalation. — [Diffbot blog](https://blog.diffbot.com/diffbots-approach-to-knowledge-graph/)

### Inferences
- The lane structure that fits a large long-tail catalog: (1) **bulk-approve lane** for high-confidence, homogeneous, previously-approved-pattern edges, approved *by rule* with the approval invalidated if the rule/model changes; (2) **group lane** for "here are 8 products, draw the boundaries / pick the relation type"; (3) **pair lane** for the low-confidence tail and for anything the model flags as variant-ambiguous; (4) **control lane** — a small random sample of auto-accepted edges, never skipped, to estimate the auto lane's precision.
- Bulk approval is only safe if it is *criteria-bound and revocable* (Tamr's model). A bulk "approve all 500" button without stored criteria creates unauditable decisions that cannot be re-validated when the model changes.
- Because the catalog is long-tail, queue volume will be dominated by a few high-degree hubs. Sorting by *change* and by *centrality* rather than by score keeps the queue proportional to actual risk.
- "Members stay put / no automatic merges / growth allowed" is a directly transplantable policy for confirmed product relations: a human-confirmed `is_accessory_for` edge should never be silently retracted by a later model run, but new SKUs may join the confirmed pattern.

### Gaps
- No public data on optimal **batch size** or session length for relation review, nor on whether group-level review is measurably faster than pairwise in ER (Zingg asserts it from design experience, without published metrics).
- No public evidence on how many reviewers are needed for semantic *relation typing* (as opposed to identity), which is a more subjective task and likely needs higher overlap than the 10–20% identity figure.
- Graphwise, Diffbot: no documented escalation or second-review mechanism at all.

## How do they prevent automation bias and semantic errors? How is reviewer feedback fed back?

### Takeaway
Automation bias is real, measurable and **not** fixed by incentives or by priming reviewers with good/bad examples. What the evidence supports is *interface* countermeasures — blind first pass, hiding the model's score until adjudication, forcing the reviewer to produce the correct answer rather than just flag an error, and measuring undercorrection separately from overcorrection. Feedback is fed back through three distinct channels that should not be conflated: labeled-pair retraining, policy/rule lock-in, and provenance invalidation.

### Cited Findings
- Randomized experiment with **2,784 participants** on reviewing AI pre-annotations. Headline: "Participants skeptical of AI detected errors more reliably and achieved higher accuracy, while those favorable toward automation more often accepted incorrect AI suggestions." — [Beck et al., Bias in the Loop (HDSR, Apr 2026)](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d); [arXiv 2509.08514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08514)
- Attitude effect was graded: annotators in the lower two quartiles of "AI liking" "correct AI-generated content more frequently… which also results in higher rates of overcorrection", while "those with a more favorable view of AI exhibit higher levels of **undercorrection**" and lower overall accuracy. — [arXiv 2509.08514](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08514v1)
- Priming with three consecutive wrong (or right) AI suggestions **did not** carry over: "The accuracy of the AI pre-annotation in the first three screens did not influence the annotations of the subsequent seven… human annotators do not form a strong and lasting impression of the pre-annotator's accuracy at the start of the task." — same source
- Performance-based pay did not help: "Offering performance-based payments to top-performing annotators does not meaningfully impact our performance metrics… accuracy does not improve with the PBP." — same source
- Requiring the reviewer to *type the correction* (rather than just flag) reduced the number of corrections and "significantly lower[ed] the rate of overcorrections" while leaving accuracy unchanged — i.e. added effort suppressed trigger-happy correction. — same source
- The dominant error mode is **semantic, not perceptual**: "accuracy is lower when identifying the pre-annotation error required a conceptual understanding of the annotation rules (**31%**)", and on one table where the pre-annotation was technically correct but required knowing market-based vs location-based emissions, "accuracy dropped to just **21%**". — same source
- Time is not a proxy for care: "neither the time spent on the whole task or just on the tutorial instruction screens is correlated with accuracy (r<0.04)"; average 71 s on the first three instances vs 48 s on the rest; 54 vs 55 s for incorrect vs correct pre-annotations. — same source
- Anti-anchoring countermeasure, stated as a deliberate design choice: "Showing it speeds review but **anchors reviewers toward the evaluator**, which inflates measured agreement. A common compromise is **blind first-pass labeling, with the evaluator score revealed only during adjudication**." — [Arize, Annotation Queue](https://arize.com/glossary/annotation-queue/)
- Agreement must be chance-corrected: report percent agreement *and* Cohen's κ (`κ = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e)`); "Persistently low chance-corrected agreement means the labels cannot yet decide between model versions. Fix the rubric, examples, or context packet and re-measure before using the labels as ground truth." — same source
- Label-quality rules from the ER side: include non-matches as well as matches; include edge cases, not just easy cases; "Label consistently across your team… If multiple people label the same kinds of pairs differently, the model receives contradictory signal"; "Use 'can't say' genuinely… rather than defaulting to 'no' when uncertain". — [Zingg, ER at Scale Part 4](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-4-thresholds-active-learning)
- Feedback channel 1 — **retraining on selected pairs**: `findTrainingData` → `label` iterated 3–5 rounds; pre-existing labels can be injected via `trainingSamples` with `z_cluster` and `z_isMatch`, but Zingg still advises running the active loop "for a few rounds to tune Zingg with the patterns it needs to learn independently". — [Zingg docs](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/running-zingg/create-training-data)
- Feedback channel 2 — **policy lock-in**: human-confirmed groups become "human-owned" and are exempt from automated merge/split, while untouched groups "stay fully dynamic". — [Zingg, A Human Corrected the AI](https://www.zingg.ai/post/human-corrected-the-ai-then-the-data-changed)
- Feedback channel 3 — **provenance invalidation**: Tamr's system treats a human approval as a provenance fact with dependencies; when an input (including the model that produced a confidence) changes, it computes "the set of descendants of all the proposed changes in the provenance DAG" and invalidates them, with modes including `PROPAGATE` — "Use the provenance information of any elements being changed in order to compute further changes whose application would make the provenance of all elements consistent." — [Tamr patent US11042523B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11042523B2/en)
- Tamr's feedback loop is framed as human-in-the-loop ML: "AI/ML mastering… through the combination of human feedback from data curators with pre-trained machine learning (ML) models and semantic comparison with large language models". — [Tamr, AI-native approach](https://cloud.docs.tamr.com/docs/about-tamrs-ai-native-approach)
- Senzing's counter-position is to remove the learning loop: principles are preconfigured, "no training, fine-tuning, or ER experts required", and the system "learns about [exceptions] in real time as new data is received" by demoting generic identifiers (e.g. an SSN used by many people "goes generic" and all prior records are re-evaluated). — [Senzing, How ER works](https://senzing.com/how-entity-resolution-works-with-senzing/)
- Human validation remains necessary even with LLM extraction: ExtracTable's evaluation found "human validation remains essential to ensure quality", with LLM output shown in an editable datagrid and missing properties rendered as a red "NOT FOUND" rather than silently omitted. — [ExtracTable (arXiv 2506.03221)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03221v3)
- Provenance as a first-class, standards-based artifact: W3C PROV defines provenance as "information about entities, activities, and people involved in producing a piece of data or thing, which can be used to form assessments about its quality, reliability or trustworthiness". — [W3C PROV-Overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)

### Inferences
- The most transferable finding for a *semantic type* review task is the 31%/21% result: reviewers fail when the judgment requires applying a taxonomy rule, not when it requires spotting a wrong string. So the UI must put the **definition of the relation type and a positive/negative example pair** next to the decision, and the rubric must be tested for κ before the queue is trusted.
- Because favourable attitudes toward AI *increase* undercorrection, the reviewer population is a design parameter: rotating reviewers, not publishing per-reviewer "approval rates" as a productivity metric, and blind first pass all reduce the drift toward rubber-stamping.
- "Can't say" must be a first-class outcome with its own queue. Collapsing it into "no" (the common shortcut) destroys the calibration signal and silently converts uncertainty into a negative label — which then trains the model to be overconfident.
- For co-purchase suggestions specifically, the model's rationale is often *the* evidence ("bought together 412 times"), and showing it up front will anchor. Blind-first-pass with rationale revealed at adjudication is the defensible default; the throughput cost should be measured, not assumed.
- Three feedback channels should be kept separate in the schema: (i) labels that retrain the scorer, (ii) confirmed edges that are policy-locked, (iii) provenance records that make past approvals revocable. Merging them into one "feedback" table is the usual cause of "the labels never leave the queue".

### Gaps
- No study found that measures automation bias specifically for **relation-typing** (as opposed to label verification); the Beck et al. task is table annotation, and the transfer is by analogy.
- No public evidence on whether showing an LLM's natural-language rationale increases or decreases automation bias relative to showing a numeric score. This is a material open question for LLM-suggested relations.
- No vendor publishes reviewer-facing guidance on how to phrase relation-type definitions to maximize inter-rater agreement.

## What metrics track review throughput, precision of suggestions, and time-to-decision?

### Takeaway
Three metric families exist and are rarely combined in one product: **resolution quality** (precision/recall/F1 against a truth set, plus MERGE/SPLIT discrepancy counts), **queue health** (completion, per-item time, per-reviewer throughput, agreement/κ), and **structural quality** (cluster density, centrality, determinism, compression). The single most important published caution is that **queue size is not a quality metric** — a small queue can mean accuracy, a narrow candidate search, or runaway auto-merging.

### Cited Findings
- **Precision / recall / F1 against a truth set**, computed by `sz_audit`: "It calculates precision, recall, and F1 scores that indicate how well Senzing is performing on the data." Worked example: precision 0.98148, recall 0.96364, F1 0.97248, with counts of Same Positives / New Positives / New Negatives and the MERGE/SPLIT review categories. — [Senzing, Auditing](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_audit/)
- Senzing's snapshot report supplies population-level health metrics per data source: **Compression** ("Percentage of records that were duplicates"), Matched Records, Matched Entities, **Ambiguous Matches**, **Possible Matches**, **Possible Relationships**, and a `principles_used` breakdown ("how many entity relationships were established at each [principle]"). A high compression ratio "often indicates data quality issues". — [Senzing, Snapshot Analysis](https://senzing.com/docs/tutorials/eda/eda_snapshot/)
- **Cluster-structure metrics** as review-prioritisation signals: `z_cluster_centrality` (matched pairs ÷ n(n−1)/2), `z_cluster_determinism`, `z_record_centrality`, `z_record_determinism`, plus `Z_MINSCORE`/`Z_MAXSCORE` per cluster; Zingg recommends filtering for "clusters that are big but loosely connected, the ones most likely to be wrong", auditing "a record with unusually high centrality [that] can falsely bridge two entities", and alerting when "the number of clusters changes out of proportion to the records added or updated". — [Zingg Explainability](https://www.zingg.ai/product/explainability); [Zingg docs, Interpret Output Scores](https://docs.zingg.ai/zingg/interpreting-results/interpret-output-scores)
- **Queue health metrics**: "Each queue tracks completion rate, average time per item, and per-annotator throughput." — [Future AGI, LLM Annotation 2026](https://futureagi.com/blog/what-is-llm-annotation-2026/)
- **Agreement metrics**: percent agreement plus Cohen's κ, with a worked example (30 items, p_o = 0.70, p_e = 0.28, κ = 0.59); "The double-reviewed overlap gives you an agreement rate. Suppose the reviewers agree on only 70% of the overlap. Then the schema or context packet needs work before you use the labels to judge an evaluator." — [Arize, Annotation Queue](https://arize.com/glossary/annotation-queue/)
- **Time-to-decision, measured**: average 71 s per item on the first three items vs 48 s on the remaining seven; 54 s vs 55 s for incorrect vs correct pre-annotations; overall task time "not correlated with accuracy (r<0.04)". Useful as a *baseline shape* (warm-up cost, then plateau) rather than a target. — [Beck et al. (arXiv 2509.08514)](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08514v1)
- **The anti-metric**: "Evaluate both accepted and rejected matches against a labeled sample. **Review queue size alone does not establish quality**: a small queue might mean accurate resolution, an overly narrow candidate search, or excessive automatic merging. Useful observations include false merges, missed aliases, disagreement by entity type, and **time spent reviewing candidates**. Choose workload-specific targets and record how the sample was labeled. **No universal rejection-rate or queue-size percentage establishes that a deployment is healthy.**" — [Neo4j Agent Memory](https://neo4j.com/labs/agent-memory/explanation/resolution-deduplication/)
- Neo4j also warns against reading the review threshold as a precision promise: "The thresholds are score cutoffs, **not guaranteed precision percentages**." — same source
- **Drift detection** as a standing metric: Zingg suggests tracking the determinism ratio across runs ("A rising determinism ratio across runs usually means your deterministic rules are improving") and alerting on disproportionate cluster-count swings. — [Zingg Explainability](https://www.zingg.ai/product/explainability)
- **Export discipline** as a metric prerequisite: "Usually the labels never leave the queue, or they come only from flagged cases. Export final labels into a versioned dataset, include an unflagged control sample, and compare the labels against evaluator scores on the same traces." Report "completion, percent agreement, and kappa" after each cycle. — [Arize, Annotation Queue](https://arize.com/glossary/annotation-queue/)
- **Audit trails as an auditable metric source**: Tamr stores curation states as versions in a DAG, so "all states can be described as ancestors or descendants of any given state", giving a temporal view of who changed what and what depended on it. — [Tamr patent US11042523B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11042523B2/en)

### Inferences
- A workable scorecard for product-relation review has four rows, and they must be read together: (1) **suggestion precision by band** (measured on the control sample of auto-accepted edges, not on the queue); (2) **queue yield** (share of reviewed candidates that were approved — a collapsing yield means the candidate generator is drifting); (3) **throughput** (items/hour and median time-to-decision, split by lane and by relation type); (4) **agreement** (κ on the double-reviewed overlap, per relation type).
- Because the catalog is long-tail, aggregate precision hides the failure: precision must be reported **per relation type and per catalog segment**, since a 95% overall precision can coexist with 60% on a rare type that carries the safety risk.
- Time-to-decision is only meaningful *within* a lane. Mixing bulk approvals into a median time-to-decision makes the number look excellent while saying nothing about the hard cases; report per-lane medians.
- The "queue size is not quality" warning implies every review system needs a **shadow sample of auto-accepted items**; without it, improvements in the queue (fewer items, higher approval rate) are indistinguishable from the model quietly auto-merging more.
- Storing reviewer decisions as provenance (who, when, on what evidence, under which rubric version) is what makes any of these metrics recomputable after a model change — and is the precondition for Tamr-style invalidation of bulk approvals.

### Gaps
- **No public benchmark numbers** for reviewer throughput in entity resolution or relation review (items/hour, cost per decision). Vendors publish architectural claims ("80% less effort" in Tamr marketing) but not measured per-item rates; the only hard per-item timings found are from a table-annotation experiment (≈48–71 s), not ER.
- No public data on precision of **LLM-suggested** relations at catalog scale, nor on the human-approval rate such suggestions achieve — so no external prior exists for sizing a review queue.
- No vendor publishes time-to-decision or reviewer-agreement metrics for their own review UI, making cross-tool comparison impossible.
- Graphwise/Ontotext and Diffbot publish no review-related metrics at all.
